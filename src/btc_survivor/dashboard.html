How to read this page

Runs. Each tab is a separate experiment. A has retail fees and $10,000 per bot, B has a 5 bps fee and $10,000, and C has retail fees and $500 so deaths come within weeks. D is B with a 4 hour horizon, added later to see trades the gate lets through. All start from identical bots.

Learners and twins. Each run has 20 small learning bots. Each one has a coin flip twin that trades at the same moments but picks long or short by coin toss, so comparing the two separates skill from luck.

Money. Every bot starts with paper money, pays real fees on a fixed $500 trade and a little rent each healthy minute, and dies when it cannot afford one more round trip and a day of rent. A dead bot is replaced in the same minute.

Feed. Bots only act in healthy minutes, when the Coinbase data is connected, fresh and consistent. Recovering or stale means everything is paused.

Stage. A launch collects 6 hours of history, waits for 30 minute outcomes to mature so predictions can be graded, trains every bot through a warmup, and only then lets them trade.

Forced trades. At these fees a bot's prediction almost never clears the cost of trading, so most trades are forced trades made every 12 hours. They are not how the bots learn. Learners train on every matured label whether or not they trade, so a trade adds no training data. Forced trades exist so costs, twins, deaths and parent eligibility can be measured.

Skill. The headline result. It is the correlation between the bots' average prediction and what Bitcoin actually did, scored once per 30 minutes after the first day, with a plain linear model beside it. The second line gives the Newey West t statistic, which must be above 2.33 to pass, the one sided 95 percent upper bound on the correlation, which adds 1.645 Newey West errors to it, and the correlation the test would find with 90 percent power at the error it really has. A test that does not pass rules out correlations above that upper bound, and when volatility clusters the bound can sit above 0.1. It is only final on day 28.

Average learner and self financing. The average learner covers the living learners only. A dead learner's slot is refilled in the same minute by a newborn holding full start money, so after deaths that average is topped up with new money. The self financing card counts every learner ever born, the living at their newest equity snapshot and the dead at their final equity less any writeoff, charges each birth its start money, and gives the outside money the births needed beyond what the dead returned. It shows the value per founding slot and its return beside buy and hold and cash. Before the first death the two cards nearly agree. They can differ a little because the average learner card reads the newest checkpoint and marks open positions at the live price, while the self financing card reads the newest equity snapshots, and either can be up to five minutes old.

Benchmarks. Buy and hold put all the money into Bitcoin at launch. Matched bought only $500 of it. Cash is start money minus rent, the bot that never trades.

Timing. The market is read as one minute candles. Each bot predicts where Bitcoin will be one horizon ahead, 30 minutes in runs A to C and 4 hours in run D, and decides once per horizon at its own minute. Learning, rent and stop checks happen every healthy minute.

Freshness. The page refreshes every 10 seconds. Bot rows come from the newest save, made every 5 minutes with every decision and fill since the one before, and the Bots header says when that was. The price and the feed state are written once a minute, so they can be a little over a minute old, and the price card says how old the price is.

Times. The runs keep UTC, and so does this page until you choose Local at the top. Local shows every time here in this computer's time zone with its abbreviation, times quoted inside feed events included, and the from and to filters below then read and show times in that zone too. A time ending in Z is read as UTC in either zone, so one copied from a log or from status can be pasted as it is, and an hour that repeats when the clocks go back is shown that way. The choice is remembered in this browser.

Decisions explorer. The Decisions section at the bottom lists every decision of the open tab, newest first, 50 to a page. Narrow it by bot, learners or twins, action, forced, gate, label, period or words in the reason. The chips count each action under the other filters, and the line below them says what share of the matured calls pointed the right way. Choose learners to score their predictions, or twins to score the coin, which should land near half. Choose a row to see its order, fills, the trade it opened or closed and how it turned out. Filters stay as the page refreshes and when you switch tabs.

Decision columns. Before is what the bot held. Prediction is the move it expected over the horizon in bps, hundredths of a percent, and a twin shows its learner's. Required edge is the move needed to pay for a round trip plus the bot's margin, and gate says whether the prediction cleared it. Forced marks a trade the timer made. A twin copies its learner's gate but never shows forced, even when it copies a forced trade. Action is what it did, where none means it stayed out and hold means it kept its position. Label is what Bitcoin really did over the horizon, marked right or wrong by whether the bot's own call pointed that way. A learner's call is its prediction and a twin's is the side its coin chose, so a twin's close or wait has no mark. It reads waiting until it matures and never when a feed gap or restart stopped it.
