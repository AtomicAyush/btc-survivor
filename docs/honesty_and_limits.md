# Honesty and limits

This page says what the six live runs can show and what they cannot, and it was written before any of them reached a result, so the reading of the results cannot be bent to fit them. Most of what happens to the bots under the default settings is decided by fees, notional and the forced timer before launch, and the page says which parts are arithmetic and which parts are open questions. The sections on learning and trading, on selection, on run C's leverage, on the self financing value and on reading a failed skill test were added on 12 October 2026 after an external review, under one healthy day into the runs and before any of them reached a result, and they change no setting. The design behind every number here is in [section 3](../PLAN.md#3-what-to-expect-from-the-default-experiment), [section 20](../PLAN.md#20-honest-limits-and-risks) and [section 21](../PLAN.md#21-controls-parallel-runs-and-success-criteria) of the plan.

## What the runs can show

The runs answer three narrow questions honestly.

* **Prediction skill.** Whether the population's mean forecast of the next 30 minute move in BTC USD correlates with what then happened, scored against predicting zero and against a plain linear model on the same inputs. This is the headline result, and it comes from run A at day 28 of healthy data.
* **Skill against luck.** Whether learners make more gross profit than coin flip twins that trade at exactly the same moments with a random direction.
* **The machinery.** That the books balance at every snapshot, that costs kill bots for the reasons the ledger says, and that death, replacement, mutation and lineage work under live conditions, which run C checks.

The success criteria and their thresholds are in [section 21 of the plan](../PLAN.md#21-controls-parallel-runs-and-success-criteria), and the tables for writing them up are in [RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md](RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md).

## What the runs cannot show

* **That a strategy makes money.** Paper fills are kinder than real ones, as the section on paper liquidity below explains, so even a profitable paper bot would not prove a profitable real one.
* **That the market holds no edge.** A failed skill test rules out, at 95 percent confidence, only correlations above the one sided upper bound that `evaluate` prints for that row. It is never read as evidence that the correlation is below about 0.1, because that bound can sit above 0.1 when the test fails, as the statistical limits below explain. A better model, more data or a longer horizon could still find something.
* **Whether evolution helps.** Runs A, B, D and E see no deaths and no selection within their windows, and selection in run C is mostly noise from the costs of forced trades. There is no control run without selection, so version 1 does not test whether mutation and selection improve the population, as the section on selection below explains.
* **Tradability from run E.** Run E, added on 12 October 2026 by the owner's decision, has no fee, slippage, short charges or margin, which no venue offers, so its bots trade on their own calls. It shows whether those calls make money before costs and how they compare with their coin flip twins and with holding cash. It cannot show whether they would survive real costs, because a real round trip costs about 12 bps at the 5 bps fee and 182 bps at retail before any margin, more than every prediction made so far, and its own trades are not a confirmatory test.
* **What a 500 dollar retail account would see.** Run C's notional is not reserved out of its cash, so its exposure grows to nearly 50 times equity before its bots die, which a spot account could not do, as the section on run C's leverage below explains.
* **Anything about other markets or other times.** Each run covers one product on one venue for 28 or 56 days of one market regime. BTC volatility was about 42 percent a year in 2025 and 46 percent so far in 2026, and the 30 days before launch were calmer than both.
* **That the best bot is good.** Among 20 bots chance alone produces one that looks impressive, so the best single bot is never evidence on its own.
* **Anything from replay.** Replay exists to test the plumbing, and replay results are never used to choose, tune or rank strategies.

The limits are listed in full in [section 20 of the plan](../PLAN.md#20-honest-limits-and-risks).

## Outcomes fixed by arithmetic

At a notional of 500 dollars the spread is one cent and walking the book costs about 0.0006 bps, so one leg of a trade costs the taker fee plus the 1 bp of extra slippage and almost nothing else. That is 4.55 dollars a leg at 90 bps and 0.30 dollars at 5 bps, so a round trip costs 9.10 dollars at retail fees and 0.60 dollars at the low fee. A bot that only trades when forced makes one round trip every 12 healthy hours, two a day, and pays 1.16 dollars a day of rent. Its burn is therefore 19.36 dollars a day at retail fees and 2.36 dollars a day at the low fee, plus small short charges of 2 bps to open and 0.5 bps an hour, which come to about 0.11 dollars for a 30 minute short.

These numbers fix the following outcomes before launch. They check the accounting, they are judged only as matched or not matched, and none of them is a finding about trading.

* **Run A.** Every open is forced and no bot dies by day 28. A learner at 10,000 dollars would take about 516 days to burn down to the death threshold. The mean equity of the founding learners should sit within about 20 dollars of 10,000 minus their rent and 9.10 dollars for each round trip they made, which at day 28 is about 55 round trips and puts them near 9,470 dollars.
* **Run B.** Almost every open is forced, and no bot dies, because even a bot that flips side at every decision would take about 334 days to die at the 5 bps fee.
* **Run C.** Every bot dies of fees. At 500 dollars the death threshold is about 10 dollars, one round trip plus a day of rent, so a quiet bot has about 25 days to live, and the arithmetic in `scripts/run_c_analysis.py`, which counts the hours before its first forced trade, puts its death on day 25.8 of healthy data. The first wave of deaths falls between days 24 and 28 and the second between days 48 and 56, and generation 2 appears in the second wave. Parent choice in run C mostly reflects market noise, because costs swamp any plausible skill a bot could have.
* **Run D.** Run D is run B with a 4 hour horizon. It burns about 2.36 dollars a day plus 4 hours of short funding per short, so no bot dies. It is exploratory and not part of the success criteria.
* **Run D2.** Run D2 is run D allowing 30 seconds of silence and 5 seconds of delay in the feed, so its costs and burn are run D's and no bot dies. It is exploratory and not part of the success criteria.
* **Run E.** Run E is run B with no fee, slippage, short charges or margin, so it is hypothetical. Its bots pay 1.16 dollars a day of rent plus half the spread and the walk through the book on each leg, and a bot dies only when its equity falls to about one day of rent, so none dies. Its required edge is only the spread and the walk through the book, about 0.001 bps at a one cent spread, which is also arithmetic. How many of its opens are its own rather than forced is not, because that depends on the predictions it makes. It is only expected, from the predictions runs A to C recorded, about 97 percent of which would pass its gate, so it is reported as descriptive and never judged as matched. It is exploratory and not part of the success criteria.

The `evaluate` command checks each of these against the database. The cost model is in [execution_model.md](execution_model.md), and the full death table is in [section 3 of the plan](../PLAN.md#3-what-to-expect-from-the-default-experiment).

## Why the gate almost never passes at retail fees

A bot opens a trade on its own only when its predicted move clears the required edge, which is the estimated round trip cost plus its margin gene. At retail fees that is two fees of 90 bps, two legs of 1 bp extra slippage, a spread and book impact that round to zero, and the starting margin of 8 bps, about 190 bps in all. A typical 30 minute move in BTC has a standard deviation of about 32 to 34 bps and an average absolute size of about 21.5 bps. Even perfect foresight would earn about 21.5 bps per 30 minute trade on average, so a 190 bps move is about six standard deviations away.

The predictions are much smaller than the moves. The output layer starts at a tenth of its normal scale, and with the learning rate of 0.0001 a learner with no signal to find keeps its predictions within a few bps, about 3 bps of spread in the synthetic check. A learner with realistic skill keeps them that small too, because an honest forecast of a noisy return stays close to zero. So the gate will not pass in practice in runs A and C, and every retail trade will be forced. At the 5 bps fee the required edge is still about 20 bps, several times the size of the predictions, so run B will be almost entirely forced as well.

This is not a bug. It is what the fees say, a retail account pays more to cross the spread twice than a 30 minute move is usually worth. The decision rule is in [the bot section of the plan](../PLAN.md#9-bots-and-trading-logic) and the learning rate check is in [learning.md](learning.md).

## Forced trades

Without forced trades a bot that never passes the gate would never trade, and nothing about its trading would ever be measured. So a flat bot that has gone `force_trade_after_hours = 12` healthy hours since its last open opens at its next decision in the direction of its current prediction, even though the gate failed. Each bot's timer starts at a random delay of up to 12 hours drawn from its own generator, so the 20 bots do not all trade in the same few minutes, and the first forced trades fall about 13 to 25 hours after launch.

Forced trades shape what the runs mean.

* Their timing comes from the timer, not from the model. Only their direction comes from the prediction.
* At retail fees they are what kills bots, at a steady 18.20 dollars a day, 18.00 of it in fees and 0.20 in extra slippage, which moves the fill price and so shows up in gross profit rather than in fees.
* Their profit before fees is a test of direction at arbitrary moments, which is exactly what the coin flip twins are built to compare.
* They are not how the bots learn. They exist so that costs, the twin comparison, deaths and parent eligibility, which needs 20 closed trades, can be measured.

Learning does not depend on trading. Once the label scale is ready, every learner that is not dead takes a training step in each healthy minute in which a label matures, whether it is flat or holds a position, and the label is the market's log return over the horizon, not the bot's own trade profit. The cost estimate reads the order book and never the bot's own fills. The one link is that each order draws its latency jitter from the learner's own generator, the same one that draws its training batches, so a trade shifts which past samples later batches draw and adds no information. The external review checked this with two identical run A learners over the same 3,000 minutes, one forced to trade every 12 hours, which made 5 opens and paid 45 dollars of fees, and one that never traded. Their weights differed by at most 0.0024, and with the jitter moved to a generator of its own they were identical bit for bit. So the confirmatory skill test, which scores every forecast window of run A whether or not any bot traded in it, gains nothing from trades and is touched by them only through those batch draws. How the bots train is in [learning.md](learning.md#learning-does-not-depend-on-trading).

Every decision row carries a `forced` flag next to the real gate result, and every trade row carries the `forced` flag too, so forced and unforced trades can always be told apart. The forced timer is described in [section 9 of the plan](../PLAN.md#9-bots-and-trading-logic).

## Selection and why evolution is not tested

Selection needs deaths, and deaths come from costs. A quiet bot at 10,000 dollars needs more than 500 days to reach its death threshold at the retail fee and more than 4,000 days at the 5 bps fee, and about 8,600 days in run E, where it pays almost nothing but rent, so runs A, B, D and E see no deaths, no replacements, no tournaments and no mutation within their windows. Every learner in them stays a founder for the whole run, and whatever they show about skill comes from learning alone.

Run C does see deaths, but every bot in it pays the same forced costs, so what separates one bot from another is the gross profit of about 50 forced round trips, which is mostly market noise. Fitness reads the same hourly equity, so parent choice is mostly noise too. The margin gene changes nothing while the gate never passes, which leaves the learning rate as the only gene that can matter. The seeded Monte Carlo in `scripts/run_c_analysis.py`, the external review's model of quiet bots run with run C's own costs and death rule, shows how little room skill has. It spaces forced opens 12 healthy hours apart, as the engine does.

| Forecast correlation | Mean death day | Standard deviation in days | Shift against no skill | Outlives a bot with no skill |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 25.83 | 0.60 | none | 50 percent of the time |
| 0.1 | 26.16 | 0.61 | 0.33 days later | 65 percent of the time |
| 0.3 | 26.85 | 0.62 | 1.02 days later | 88 percent of the time |

The verifier spaced forced opens 12.25 hours apart, which gives means of 26.32, 26.66 and 27.36 days, about half a day later, with nearly the same spread, shifts and shares.

A correlation of 0.1, about what the skill test is built to detect, moves the mean death day by about half a standard deviation of the noise. Version 1 therefore does not test whether evolution helps. There is no control run whose dead bots are replaced without selection, and a single such run started later would mix selection with a different market window, with inherited weights and with a child's shorter warmup, so it would have almost no power. After the external review it was decided to record this limit rather than test it, and no control run was added. Run C remains a check that death, replacement, mutation and lineage work under live conditions, and `scripts/run_c_analysis.py` also compares each parent's skill with that of the other learners eligible to be parents at every mutated birth, where a null result is expected. Fitness and tournaments are described in [evolution.md](evolution.md#why-fees-decide-survival-at-retail-costs), and the tables for the run C analyses are in [RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md](RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md#run-c-analyses).

## Run C trades on unreserved leverage

Opening a position takes only the fee, and for a short the opening charge, out of cash. The broker checks the book, the position and the quantity before a fill and never the bot's funds, the notional stays fixed at 500 dollars, and the only funds check is the one at start up that the notional is no larger than start equity. So no principal or margin is reserved, and as equity falls the exposure grows. In runs A, B, D and E equity of about 9,400 dollars or more covers 500 many times over, while run C starts at 1 times. On copies of the databases taken at `18:45` UTC on 12 October 2026, the largest notional over equity at any open was 0.05 times in runs A and B, run D had not opened yet, and run C's first 13 learner opens reached at most 1.00 times before the fee and 1.01 times after.

From the config alone, a run C bot that trades only when forced and never gains or loses on the market carries 2 times its equity after the open fee on day 13.8, 5 times on day 21.3, 10 times on day 23.8 and 24.7 times on day 25.3. Its last open, on day 25.8 at 15.08 dollars of equity, is 33.2 times before the fee and 47.5 times after, and it leaves the bot just above its 10.26 dollar death threshold, so it holds that position for 30 minutes before the close fee kills it. Nothing like a margin call exists, so the death rule is the only check on a bot's funds. A spot account with no margin could not open even run C's first trade, because 500 dollars plus a 4.50 dollar fee is more than the 500 it holds. Under an illustrative 5 times margin cap a quiet bot would stop trading near day 22 and live on rent until day 92.7 instead of dying on day 25.8, so run C's death schedule depends on this leverage.

Run C's dollar paths and death days are therefore not what a 500 dollar retail account would see, while its purpose of checking death, replacement, mutation and lineage still holds. The skill test and the twin test use a fixed notional and are unaffected. The mechanism and the whole path are in [execution_model.md](execution_model.md#no-principal-is-reserved).

## Paper liquidity and other kindnesses

Paper trading is kinder than real trading in ways that cannot be fully modelled.

* **Orders never consume liquidity.** A learner and its twin trade at the same moment and both fill against the same price levels, no paper order ever moves the price another bot sees, and nobody else in the market reacts to them.
* **There is no queue and no rejection by the exchange.** An order fills against the book as it stands after the simulated latency of 1,000 milliseconds plus up to 300 milliseconds of jitter. It is rejected when the top 20 levels cannot fill it, when the feed leaves Healthy first or when the process restarts.
* **Fees never change during a run.** A fixed 90 bps is harsher than reality after the first weeks, because a real account trading about 2,000 dollars a day would reach the 75 bps tier in about 5 days and 30 bps in about 25 days.
* **No money is set aside for a position.** An open takes only its fee out of cash and the broker never checks buying power, which is harmless at 10,000 dollars and lets run C reach nearly 50 times its equity, as the section above explains.
* **The short leg is a composite.** It pays the Coinbase spot taker fee plus Kraken style margin charges, a mix no single account would pay. Kraken says Coinbase US does not offer spot margin shorting, and that could not be checked with Coinbase. The Kraken style charges add 2.25 bps to a 30 minute short's round trip, 184.25 bps against 182 for a long at the retail fee, so the mix barely moves costs, and the skill test uses no costs at all.
* **The low fee profile is out of reach for a US retail account.** The 5 bps tier needs 20 million dollars of volume a month, so a good result in run B says the learning loop works, not that a retail account could trade it. The Intro taker fee is 50 bps in the EU, the UK and Canada and 10 bps in the rest of the world, so the account's region matters.

The extra slippage of 1 bp per leg is larger than anything the book walk produced at 500 dollars, which pushes the other way. The fill model and its sources are in [execution_model.md](execution_model.md).

## The coin flip twins and their power

Every learner slot also holds a twin with its own account. Whenever the learner opens, closes or flips, the twin does the same at the same moment, choosing long or short with a fair coin. The twin pays the same fees, slippage, latency, funding and rent, has its own stop loss and death check, is never a parent and never counts toward fitness. Because the pair trade at the same moments, the only difference between them is the direction, so a gap between them measures direction skill rather than timing luck.

Criterion 2 pairs each closed learner trade with its twin's trade from the same decision and compares gross profit before fees, funding and short charges, since those differ only through the direction mix. The pairs are grouped into fixed 12 hour blocks of the UTC clock, which keeps the test valid when trades of different bots overlap in time. A 28 day run has about 56 blocks, a few more when gaps stretch its 28 healthy days over more calendar days. The test passes when the mean of the block means is positive and its t statistic is above the one sided 1 percent quantile of Student's t, with one degree of freedom fewer than the number of blocks.

The twin's coin adds noise as well as removing timing luck. At a forecast correlation of about 0.1 this test has only about a third of the power of the skill test, so it is supporting evidence, and a failure of this test alone does not contradict a pass of the skill test. In run C the twins die of fees on about the same schedule as the learners, and a dead twin is replaced by a fresh twin in the same slot. The twins are described in [section 21 of the plan](../PLAN.md#21-controls-parallel-runs-and-success-criteria) and their code is in [controls.py](../src/btc_survivor/controls.py).

## Population averages and the self financing value

The living mean in the `benchmark` table, which the dashboard shows as the average living learner and draws as the mean living learner, counts only the learners alive at that minute. A dead learner's slot is refilled in the same minute by a newborn holding full start equity, so after deaths that mean is topped up with new money and says little about how the population has done. The figure `status` prints, all learners ever born holding so much of the capital given, does count the dead, but its capital grows with every birth, and a dead learner's final equity is recorded after any negative cash has been written off, so writeoffs are left out.

The figure to set against buy and hold and cash is the self financing value. It counts every learner ever born, a living one at its newest equity snapshot and a dead one at its final equity less any writeoff, and charges every replacement birth its start equity, as an investor who funded the founders once and paid for every birth would see it. The outside money is the most that the dead learners' residuals, less the cost of the births, ever fell below zero, the money the births needed from elsewhere. In the external review's reproduction on the test suite's deadly settings, 4 slots of 100 dollars at a 900 bps fee over 3 synthetic hours, 32 learners were born and 28 died. At the end the living mean was 77.42 dollars while the value per slot was `-554.46` dollars, and 2,527.51 dollars of outside money had been needed on a 400 dollar budget. Twins are left out of both figures. Before the first death the value per slot equals the living mean, as it did in all four runs on 12 October 2026.

`scripts/population_value.py` prints the value per slot, its return and the outside money beside buy and hold and cash, at the end of every healthy day, from the same code as the dashboard's self financing card. Run C's first deaths, expected from about day 24, are where the two figures part. The definition is in [`self_financing.py`](../src/btc_survivor/cli/self_financing.py), and its table is in [RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md](RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md#self-financing-value).

## Statistical limits

* **One confirmatory test.** Only the skill test on run A at day 28 counts. It uses one forecast per 30 minute window so labels never overlap, skips the first day of forecasts, and uses a Newey West standard error over 12 windows, about six hours, with a one sided 1 percent level. Forecasts begin about 7 hours after launch, once the label scale is ready, so day 28 brings about 1,280 scored windows, fewer after gaps. At that count it can detect a correlation of about 0.1 with 90 percent power if the windows are independent. `evaluate` prints the realized window count and two detectable correlations, that figure, 3.61 over the square root of the window count, and a second one, 3.61 times the realized Newey West error. The second is the honest one, and it is usually the larger when volatility clusters in both the forecast and the label. In the external review's simulation with 1,280 windows and shared volatility clustering, the real detectable correlation was 0.147 while the first figure said 0.101.
* **A failed test.** `evaluate` and the dashboard also print a one sided 95 percent upper bound, the correlation plus 1.645 Newey West errors. A test that fails rules out only correlations above that bound. In the same simulation at a true correlation of 0.1, the test failed 10.4 percent of the time with independent errors, and 55 percent of those failures still had an upper bound above 0.1. With shared volatility clustering it failed 48.1 percent of the time, and 87 percent of the failures had an upper bound above 0.1. So a failure is reported with its upper bound and is never read as evidence that the correlation is below about 0.1. The pass rule, a Newey West t above 2.33, is unchanged and stays valid either way.
* **Overlapping labels.** Consecutive one minute labels share almost all of their 30 minutes. The rolling skill of a single bot over 360 samples rests on only about 12 independent outcomes, so the skill column in `status` is noisy by nature.
* **Squared error.** With no skill the population's squared error is expected to come out a few percent worse than predicting zero, by about the square of the forecast's spread over the label's. A small loss against zero is therefore not evidence of harmful predictions.
* **Shared labels.** Runs B and C learn from the same market as run A, so they are reported beside it and are not separate tests.
* **Run D.** With about 6 windows a day, its skill test at day 28 can only detect a correlation of about 0.28. All 20 of its bots decide within the first 20 minutes of each 4 hour block, so its trades cluster in time. Its numbers are descriptive.
* **Run D2.** It has run D's limits and adds one. It was added because short stalls discard most of run D's 4 hour labels, and it shows whether tolerating them keeps the labels and learning going. Its bots may decide, fill and learn through up to 30 seconds of silence or 5 seconds of delay, a real loosening, while a pause in which Coinbase holds back more than about 6 seconds of stream, a disconnect or a restart still ends its epochs. Its forecasts learn from the same market as run D's, so the comparison between them is descriptive.
* **Run E.** Its trades are its own calls, but the run is hypothetical and exploratory, and its forecasts learn from the same market as the other runs, so its numbers are descriptive and none of them is a confirmatory test.
* **Early days.** Results at days 7 and 14 are descriptive, only the final day counts, and no setting changes during a run.
* **Healthy days, not calendar days.** The criteria count days of healthy data. Feed gaps, `clock_skew` pauses and a sleeping Mac all stop the count, so day 28 arrives later than 28 calendar days after launch.

The tests are defined in [section 21 of the plan](../PLAN.md#21-controls-parallel-runs-and-success-criteria) and their code is in [stats.py](../src/btc_survivor/cli/stats.py).

## Settings across restarts

No setting changes during a run, but the code enforces only part of that. At launch a run writes the whole text of its config file into the `meta` table under `config_toml`, next to `master_seed`, and a checkpoint holds no copy of the config. On resume the engine compares only the master seed, the population size and the twin setting, so a fee, a horizon or any other setting edited before a restart would change a running experiment without a warning. `scripts/check_config_integrity.py` compares each config file with the text its run stored and prints match, the same settings when only comments or layout differ, logging only when only `run.log_level` differs, or every setting that differs with both values. Its exit status is 0 when every run matches, holds the same settings or differs only in `run.log_level`, 1 when any other setting differs, and 2 when a database or its stored config is missing. On 12 October 2026 all four runs matched their files exactly. The check is run before any restart, as the restart rules in [operations.md](operations.md#starting-runs) require, and recorded at each write up, as [RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md](RESULTS_TEMPLATE.md#config-integrity) sets out.

## A null result is a real finding

If the skill test fails, the runs still say something concrete. Correlations of the population's forecasts of 30 minute BTC moves above the printed 95 percent upper bound are ruled out for those weeks, whatever that bound turns out to be, and a small or zero correlation fits the near zero autocorrelation of 15 and 30 minute returns found while planning and the gross edges of about 0.5 to 5 bps per trade that older studies suggest. If every bot in run C dies of fees, that is what the costs predicted, and the `deaths` table shows which of fees, funding, rent, stop losses and other trading losses killed each one.

Every run is reported whatever it shows, and no default was tuned to make the bots look better. A null result is the honest answer to the question version 1 asks, and it is the baseline any later predictor has to beat. Version 2 runs as a separate system that paper trades seven big tech stocks and the QQQ fund, and tests whether news scored by language models forecasts their next 30 minute return, under one confirmatory skill test fixed before launch that adapts these rules to stocks, as set out in [PLAN_V2.md](../PLAN_V2.md), its [confirmatory test](../PLAN_V2.md#115-the-confirmatory-test) and its [road map](../PLAN_V2.md#117-road-map-after-version-2).
